How sure is this?

Not very, and we would rather say so up front. Everything on this site is one language model's estimate of what AI could do to a list of skills. It is a way to think about the shape of a job, not a measurement of anyone's job.

What was scored

All — skills in the ESCO classification were scored, one at a time. A language model read the skill's own ESCO description and gave it two numbers from 1 to 10:

  • Automation risk — how much of this skill a machine could take over.
  • Amplification — how much more a person could do with AI helping them do it.

Both numbers are about the skill as ESCO describes it. No workplace was observed, no worker was asked, and no employment data went into them.

What was scored

All — skills in the ESCO classification were scored, one at a time, by a judgment model (—) that answers with a probability for each level of an answer rather than with a number it made up. It read the skill's own ESCO description and nothing else.

Every question that mentions AI shares one preamble, so that “the system” means the same thing in all of them: software that reads and writes text, code, tables, documents and images and can call other software, with no body, no hands, no eyes in a room and no physical presence.

Six questions per skill:

Digital output
Is the result of this work something that exists as data or a document?
AI substitution
How much of the work can that system carry out itself, from none of it to all of it, unsupervised?
Machine automation
AI set aside: how much of the work does physical equipment do — production lines, CNC machines, robots, conveyors, process plant?
AI assistance
If the person keeps the work, how much better does it get with that system beside them, from no difference to several times as much work?
Mode
How the skill is mainly exercised: on things, with people, directing others, through software, or on paper in place.
Deployment
Whether such systems are in routine use, available, demonstrated, or not shown. This one is descriptive only; it never decides a class.

Knowledge items get a variant of the two “how much of the work” questions that asks about the work the knowledge is applied in, rather than about the knowledge itself. No workplace was observed, no worker was asked, and no employment data went in.

How a skill gets its class

Each set of answers becomes a probability rather than an average: that the system could take the work over, that the person gets a clear gain across most of it, that machinery does it. Each is compared with the same cut, —. The first rule that matches wins, so every scored skill has exactly one class.

How a job gets its two scores

An occupation in ESCO is a list of skills, split into essential and optional ones. Its two scores are the weighted average of its skills' scores, with essential skills counting double. That is the whole calculation. There is no separate model pass for occupations, which is why the same — occupations come out the same everywhere on this site.

An occupation with no scored skills gets no scores at all, and shows as “Not scored” rather than as a zero or a question mark.

How a job gets four shares and one of seven types

An occupation in ESCO is a list of skills, split into essential and optional ones. Essential skills count double. Add up that weight per class and you get four shares that sum to one: how much of the job is work AI can take over, how much AI assists, how much machines can do, and how much stays human. Skills with no scores are left out, and an occupation with no scored skills at all shows as “Not scored” rather than as a zero.

There is no cut-off on a pair of numbers here. The four shares are put through seven rules, and the first that matches decides the type — which is why the order of the table below is the order they are tried in.

Two choices made after the run, stated openly

Every threshold above was fixed before the full run, with two exceptions that the run's own distribution forced. Neither was tuned to make a particular job land anywhere; both are structural, and both are worth knowing about before you trust a type.

  • Assistance is read at the third level, not the second. The design first counted any clear gain on part of the work. On the whole set that made most skills “AI assists” and put roughly six occupations in seven into a single type — a classification that says the same thing about almost everything. Read at the level that asks for a clear gain across most of the work, the types spread out. The looser reading is still published beside each skill, so the choice can be revisited without re-scoring anything.
  • The first rule lost a condition. It used to require a low spread across a job's skills as well as a high substituted share. But that share is built from threshold probabilities, which sit close to nought or close to one by construction, so the spread is largest exactly where the substituted share is high but not total. Translators, accountants and data entry clerks fell through to Mixed because of it. The spread is still published; it no longer gates a type.

Two more readings, so the labels are not read as more than they are. “Machines can do” is a floor: the wording of the machinery question is deliberately conservative, so where that share is large the real figure is larger still. And “Stays human” does not mean AI is of no help — many of those skills still get a clear gain on part of the work, under the level this rubric counts as assistance.

Why there are two models of scoring here

The switch at the top of the page offers two score sets, and they are not two runs of the same thing. The first rubric asked one question per axis — how likely AI is to automate a skill, how much AI can amplify it — averaged a job's skills and cut the two averages at 6. Measured on its own output that design had three problems worth fixing.

  • AI and machinery were the same axis. A welding robot and a language model both pushed the automation score up, which are very different claims about a job. They are now separate questions, and machine automation is reported on its own.
  • Knowledge was scored like activity. “Tax law” is not a task anyone performs. Knowledge items are now asked about the work the knowledge is applied in.
  • Averaging flattened everything, and the cut-off did the classifying. A job is now described by the shares of its skills across four classes, so the shape of the work survives the roll-up instead of collapsing into one number either side of a line.

Both sets stay published, because neither is ground truth and a reader is entitled to see how much the answer depends on the question. The full specification of the newer one, including the exact wording and the thresholds, is in scoring-v2.md on GitHub.

What this means for you

Trust the direction and the ranking more than the box. That one job scores higher on automation risk than another is the sturdier part of this data; which side of a line each one fell is the fragile part.

If your job sits near a line, the honest answer is that it could go either way. And a 0.2 difference between two jobs is not a difference worth acting on.

What this means for you

Trust the shape more than the label. That a job carries a large “AI can take over” share and a small one for machinery is the sturdier part of this data; which of seven types the rules then handed it is the fragile part, and for a job near a cut-off it could genuinely go either way.

A large substituted share is not a verdict on anybody. It says which part of the week is the first candidate for redesign, and it leaves three other shares — the assisted part, the mechanised part and the part that stays human — as the ones worth growing.

What these scores are not

  • They are not a measurement. Nobody watched a real job being done.
  • There is no ground truth here. Nothing was checked against an outcome, because there is no dataset of what AI has actually done to these occupations.
  • They are not a forecast of employment. Nothing here says how many people will be doing a job, what it will pay, or when anything happens.
  • They are not advice about a person. An occupation is an average of a skill list; the job you actually do is not.

What would make this better

  • Task-level time studies. Real measurements of how long the tasks in a job take, and which of them AI tools shorten, would replace the estimate with an observation.
  • Employer data on what AI actually replaced. Which tasks moved to a tool, and what the people who used to do them do now.
  • A rubric that separates “a machine already does this” from “AI could do this”. Today both push the automation score up, and they are very different claims about a job.
  • Answers checked against the people who do the work. The model's judgments have never been put to a nurse, a welder or a bookkeeper. Even a small panel saying where the shares are wrong would be worth more than another model's opinion.
