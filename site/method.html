How sure is this?

Not very, and we would rather say so up front. Everything on this site is one language model's estimate of what AI could do to a list of skills. It is a way to think about the shape of a job, not a measurement of anyone's job.

What was scored

All — skills in the ESCO classification were scored, one at a time. A language model read the skill's own ESCO description and gave it two numbers from 1 to 10:

  • Automation risk — how much of this skill a machine could take over.
  • Amplification — how much more a person could do with AI helping them do it.

Both numbers are about the skill as ESCO describes it. No workplace was observed, no worker was asked, and no employment data went into them.

How a job gets its two scores

An occupation in ESCO is a list of skills, split into essential and optional ones. Its two scores are the weighted average of its skills' scores, with essential skills counting double. That is the whole calculation. There is no separate model pass for occupations, which is why the same — occupations come out the same everywhere on this site.

An occupation with no scored skills gets no scores at all, and shows as “Not scored” rather than as a zero or a question mark.

What the four boxes mean

The two averages are compared with one cut-off, —, on both axes. That gives four boxes. The cut-off is hard: a job at 5.9 and a job at 6.1 land in different boxes although almost nothing separates them.

What this means for you

Trust the direction and the ranking more than the box. That one job scores higher on automation risk than another is the sturdier part of this data; which side of a line each one fell is the fragile part.

If your job sits near a line, the honest answer is that it could go either way. And a 0.2 difference between two jobs is not a difference worth acting on.

What these scores are not

  • They are not a measurement. Nobody watched a real job being done.
  • There is no ground truth here. Nothing was checked against an outcome, because there is no dataset of what AI has actually done to these occupations.
  • They are not a forecast of employment. Nothing here says how many people will be doing a job, what it will pay, or when anything happens.
  • They are not advice about a person. An occupation is an average of a skill list; the job you actually do is not.

What would make this better

  • Task-level time studies. Real measurements of how long the tasks in a job take, and which of them AI tools shorten, would replace the estimate with an observation.
  • Employer data on what AI actually replaced. Which tasks moved to a tool, and what the people who used to do them do now.
  • A rubric that separates “a machine already does this” from “AI could do this”. Today both push the automation score up, and they are very different claims about a job.

Where the data comes from

ESCO v1.2.1
The European Commission's classification of occupations and skills. Every occupation, skill, description and essential/optional relation on this site comes from ESCO. The scores do not.
ISCO-08
The ILO's International Standard Classification of Occupations, which ESCO follows. It supplies the major, sub-major, minor and unit groups used to browse sectors.
Google Gemini Flash
The language model that read each skill description and produced the two scores and the written rationale shown on every skill page.
